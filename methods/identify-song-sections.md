# Identify song sections

## Key takeaway

Compare what returns and what changes, then use those observations to propose section boundaries and roles.

## Summary

Use this listening analysis to orient yourself in a pop/rock song before learning phrases or planning a performance. The output is a map of the selected version: section locations, proposed labels, supporting observations and unresolved boundaries. This is a reusable adaptation of published form analysis, not a newly invented classification or a tested training protocol.

## Inputs for comparison

Use an identified recording you can pause and replay. Words from an authorised source or a matching score can help; notation reading and chord naming are not required. Record the performer/version and link or edition. Another arrangement can have different repeats, introductions or endings. Without accessible audio, analyse available text as text and leave musical observations unverified.

A **phrase** is a connected musical unit; a **section** groups phrases into a larger part. [Verse and chorus](../concepts/verse-and-chorus.md) describe section roles: verses commonly develop changing text; choruses return to a central message. A recurring line inside a section can be a refrain. Sharing a melody does not by itself identify a chorus.

## Why this operation helps

Comparing separated occurrences lets you distinguish a returning musical pattern from a new textual situation. Keeping those observations separate makes the proposed role explainable; replaying around a boundary checks whether the chosen start actually locates that passage. These are reasons for the analysis, not evidence that a particular listening schedule improves learning.

Choose this analysis when you need to locate returns and transitions. If you only need the entrance to one phrase, a clear supplied cue may be sufficient; a full section map would add unnecessary work. An existing annotated score can provide provisional labels, but check that it matches the recording.

## Procedure for a provisional map

1. **Orient yourself.** Listen through once. Notice the vocal entrance, large returns or contrasts, instrumental passages and ending. Jot down a few candidate locations without assigning every role. If the whole song is overwhelming, start with one suspected return and expand later.
2. **Examine a possible boundary.** Replay from before the candidate location through the following phrase. Note what suggests a new part: a completed phrase group, a new textual situation, a change in the vocal pattern or accompaniment, or a return of earlier material. Mark a playback location or score position you can find again. A pause or louder moment alone is insufficient; keep unclear transitions provisional.
3. **Compare passages directly.** Alternate two suspected counterparts, including enough of each to hear its phrases. Note separately whether the words, melody and rhythmic pattern recur or change. Add accompaniment or harmony observations only when you can identify them. This prevents similar words from standing in for similar music, or repeated music from implying identical text. When memory is unclear, replay rather than inventing a difference.
4. **Propose the roles.** Consider how each passage contributes to the whole. Changing situations across a recurring musical framework may support verse labels; a returning section centred on the same appeal may support chorus labels. Check the whole passage, not just the title. Verse and chorus can share chords or even melody. If these labels do not fit, use a neutral description such as opening section, contrasting section or instrumental passage until a more suitable analysis is available. Do not force every song into verse–chorus form.
5. **Check the map in sequence.** Play through the mapped region and locate each proposed start again. Compare all occurrences given the same label. Keep meaningful variations visible, and distinguish an uncertain start from an uncertain role. If evidence conflicts, revise the grouping or retain alternatives; consult a matching score or informed demonstration when needed. For a whole-song map, include the introduction, intervening passages and ending actually heard; otherwise label the map as partial.

## Checks and uncertainty

Use notes or a small table, whichever is easier to read. Keep enough information to recover the location, explain the label and identify what needs another listen. A useful map lets you find the intended passage again and justify its grouping without relying only on the supplied label. If you cannot, identify whether the unresolved issue is location, the comparison or the role, and repeat that part of the analysis. A confidently written label is not additional evidence. Agreement with an annotation is a cross-check, not proof that it fits every version.

## Worked reasoning example

These observations are invented to demonstrate the decision, not taken from a recording:

| Location | Observed relationship | Provisional role |
|---|---|---|
| First vocal section | Describes a departure using one phrase pattern | Verse 1 |
| Following section | Presents a central appeal with a contrasting pattern | Chorus candidate |
| Later vocal section | Describes an arrival using the first pattern | Verse 2 |
| Following return | Repeats the appeal and second pattern | Supports the chorus interpretation |

The later return strengthens the earlier chorus hypothesis. Repeated music in the two verses does not invalidate their labels because their words develop different situations. If only one line of the supposed chorus returned inside each verse, reconsider whether it is a refrain rather than a separate section. This example supplies observations; a real analysis must obtain them through listening or identify them as supplied by an annotation.

## Source basis and limits

[Open Music Theory](../references/open-music-theory-undated-pop-rock-form.md) demonstrates comparing recurring passages and recording a section timeline. Its terminology separates changes in words from changes in music; its notation distinguishes musical content from function. Those are the adopted analytical operations. The five-step listening order, beginner simplifications, neutral labels and uncertainty decisions above are our adaptation, not a sequence prescribed by the source. No improvement in learner recognition has been demonstrated here.

Section analyses can differ, and this account does not cover every musical tradition or every pop/rock formal function. A text annotation cannot verify an audio boundary. A usable map helps locate what to practise; it does not establish accurate singing. The [song-learning Practice](../practices/learn-and-perform-a-song.md#stage2-understand-the-whole-song) applies the operation to a supported comparison before asking for independent recognition.
