# Singing Body of Knowledge Outline

## Key takeaway

Understand singing through directly named topics, with each subsystem explained by its scope, components, relationships and interfaces.

## Summary

This beginner outline lists singing topics at one level. Subsystem descriptions sit beside their coverage requirements, existing explanations and gaps. Interfaces show how systems connect; overlap is useful and does not disqualify a subject from being a subsystem. Boundaries are working explanatory choices grounded in established voice-pedagogy and music-theory subjects.

The outline defines what needs to be understood and to what depth. Concepts, Principles, Models, Methods and Practices remain the knowledge roles. A Model represents a coherent system; a Practice selects and combines knowledge for an outcome. A topic heading does not require a separate Model file. Available activities remain in the [README](../README.md#start-here).

## Purpose and scope

**Audience:** beginners learning to understand singing and approach a suitable song. No notation reading, prior training or fixed vocal range is assumed. Learning by listening and imitation is included; Western notation and sight singing form an optional reading branch.

**Target:** explain what a singing task asks, distinguish pitch, timing, words and expression, and make informed learning choices. Required capabilities below are goals, not demonstrated learner results or a compulsory lesson sequence. The current Practices still implement only a first contour/rhythm task and single-reference pitch matching.

The existing [starting conditions](../practices/first-singing-session.md#starting-conditions) govern vocal activities. Do not sing through pain, hoarseness or unusual fatigue; persistent voice problems call for clinical advice. Comfort is an observation, not medical clearance. The [voice-care Reference](../references/nidcd-2025-taking-care-of-your-voice.md) supplies the existing boundaries. This outline adds no vocal exercise or treatment.

Clinical diagnosis, rehabilitation, professional teacher preparation, exhaustive anatomy/acoustics, advanced range development, specialised belt/distortion techniques, professional production and comprehensive coverage of vocal traditions are outside this edition. Individual and developing voices, repertoire suitability and style remain required topics; they do not receive generic prescriptions here.

## How to read the outline

Topics appear directly at the same heading level. Each section states its scope, components and relationships, interfaces, and an example or question, followed by required knowledge and coverage gaps.

A **minimal subsystem** is a small, coherent explanatory unit for a stated question. It has a boundary, interacting components or defined relationships, and interfaces with other systems. Minimal does not mean isolated or anatomically indivisible. For example, a Phrasing Model can explain boundaries, emphasis and timing while connecting to Respiration and Diction; those interfaces do not make it less of a system.

A heading identifies necessary knowledge, not a completed Model or proof of minimality. Some topics still need fuller accounts or more focused Model boundaries. Physical functions, musical representations and coordination loops remain different kinds of explanation. Initial pitch and tonal accounts use explicitly scoped Western conventions; other traditions require their own accounts. Order is for navigation, not a lesson sequence.

## Reading the coverage tables

**Understand** states the needed knowledge; **Be able to do** states introductory depth. **Partial** means an existing explanation contributes without completing the topic. **Source only** means a relevant external account is recorded but a locally sufficient explanation is missing. **Missing** identifies an unsupported local gap. A planned Model counts as missing until authored; a source label or passing structural check does not establish competence or scientific validation.

## Respiration

**Scope:** breath supply and its coordination with vocal demands; detailed laryngeal vibration belongs to Phonation.

**Components and relationships:** respiratory movement, pressure and airflow.

**Interfaces:** [Phonation](#phonation) concerns vibration supplied by airflow; [Phrasing](#phrasing) concerns phrase demands and boundaries.

**Example:** phrase demands connect musical grouping to breath use; explaining a short phrase should distinguish its breath requirement from its intended pitch. A complete respiratory Model is missing.

## Phonation

**Scope:** the generation, continuation and ending of voiced sound; the vocal tract shapes the resulting source.

**Components and relationships:** vocal folds, airflow and vibration.

**Interfaces:** [Respiration](#respiration) concerns air supply; [Vocal tract resonance](#vocal-tract-resonance) concerns acoustic shaping; [Registration](#registration) concerns register accounts and transitions.

**Example:** an easy hum and a spoken vowel both involve voicing, but that does not establish identical articulation or healthy technique. [Registration](#registration) connects these functions across pitches and transitions; its labels are not pitch names.

| Phonation and sound continuity | Starting, sustaining and ending voiced sound; relationship to airflow and articulation | Identify the intended start and release of a phrase and explain which parts of voice function are involved. | **Partial:** voice-production Concept; onset, release and continuity explanations missing. |

## Vocal tract resonance

**Scope:** acoustic shaping, distinguished from source generation and from the movements that establish a tract configuration.

**Components and relationships:** excitation, vocal-tract configuration, resonances and resulting spectrum.

**Interfaces:** [Phonation](#phonation) concerns the source; [Articulation](#articulation) concerns movements that establish tract configurations; [Registration](#registration) connects production and acoustic questions.

**Example:** compare two vowels at the same intended pitch to ask which differences concern sound shaping. This is a proposed comparison, not a recording analysis or a full source–filter lesson.

## Articulation

**Scope:** configurations and movements used to form speech sounds; text meaning and musical placement are handled at its interfaces.

**Components and relationships:** tongue, lips, jaw and other articulators in vowel/consonant formation.

**Interfaces:** [Vocal tract resonance](#vocal-tract-resonance) concerns acoustic consequences; [Diction and meaning](#diction-and-meaning) concerns language-specific pronunciation; [Text setting](#text-setting) concerns timing in music.

**Example:** a vowel change can involve articulation and resonance together; neither description replaces the other. Language-specific inventories still need sources.

## Pitch relationships

**Scope:** the height, class and distance relationships of musical pitches under a declared convention. Tonal function and vocal production are outside this unit.

**Components and relationships:** individual pitches, note labels, octave positions, octave-equivalent classes and intervals.

**Interfaces:** [Auditory vocal pitch matching](#auditory-vocal-pitch-matching) compares a target with an attempt; [Tonal organisation](#tonal-organisation) assigns contextual roles; [Rhythmic organisation](#rhythmic-organisation) supplies event timing.

**Example:** A3 and A4 share a class but differ by an octave; a unison-matching task needs the height, not just the class. The [Pitch relationships Model](../models/pitch-relationships.md) supplies a focused representation.

## Rhythmic organisation

**Scope:** when sound events and silences occur and how long they last against a pulse; beat grouping belongs to Metrical organisation.

**Components and relationships:** onsets, durations, rests, pulse and tempo.

**Interfaces:** [Metrical organisation](#metrical-organisation) concerns beat grouping; [Text setting](#text-setting) aligns syllables with events; [Phrasing](#phrasing) concerns grouping and expressive timing.

**Example:** three hums and a silent fourth beat contain four pulse positions but only three vocal events. Changing the pitches need not change that timing pattern.

## Text setting

**Scope:** the alignment of text with musical events, not the entire language or interpretation of a song.

**Components and relationships:** syllables, word stress, consonant/vowel timing, note events and musical accents.

**Interfaces:** [Articulation](#articulation) concerns speech-sound formation; [Diction and meaning](#diction-and-meaning) concerns pronunciation and stress; [Rhythmic organisation](#rhythmic-organisation) supplies musical timing.

**Example:** a constructed two-syllable word can occupy two notes, or one syllable can extend over several; the mapping needs more information than a pitch list. Detailed accounts and language-specific examples still need source inspection.

## Notation and sight singing

**Scope:** Optional reading coverage connecting written representations to sound. Reading is not a prerequisite for learning by listening and imitation.

**Components and relationships:** Written pitches, durations, rests and directions; reading relates these representations to the intended sound.

**Interfaces:** [Pitch relationships](#pitch-relationships), [Rhythmic organisation](#rhythmic-organisation), [Metrical organisation](#metrical-organisation) and [Phrasing](#phrasing) supply the relationships represented; reading and vocal coordination remain distinguishable.

**Example question:** Is a difficulty in a new passage about interpreting its notation, hearing its target or producing it?

## Maintaining the outline

Start with established accounts and the understanding needed for an intended Practice. List topics directly. For a proposed subsystem, state its scope, components, relationships, interfaces and example; keep uncertain boundaries provisional. Connections to other systems do not require a separate topic category. A Model must provide an actual representation and worked interpretation, not only reproduce the outline.

Keep source scope, topic depth, existing explanations and gaps together. Preserve useful overlap and necessary coverage without requiring one Model per heading. Update the overview and any retained supporting pictures when labels or meaning change. One overall illustration is required; additional pictures remain optional and user-selected. Embed images without redundant full-size links. The outline stays maintained Markdown and requires no generator.
