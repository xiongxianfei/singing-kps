# Learn and perform a song

## Key takeaway

Prepare a suitable song by understanding the whole, learning phrases, addressing specific difficulties and returning to a complete performance.

## Summary

The target is to sing a chosen arrangement with recognisable melody, reliable timing, understandable words and an intended expression, while respecting vocal comfort. A phrase is a musical unit heard as a connected thought; it need not equal one printed lyric line or one breath. This Practice connects pitch, rhythm, words and interpretation through a real song. It can span several sessions; finishing a stage does not oblige you to continue singing.

The worked example is **Stand by Me, associated with Ben E. King's recording**. Begin with a verse and chorus as a milestone, then prepare the remaining sections and the ending of your chosen arrangement. The example supplies decisions and possible difficulties, not a transcription, assigned key or report of your ability. No notation reading or previous completion of our humming and single-note tasks is required.

## Starting conditions

Have access to a clear melody reference and the words for the version you intend to sing, through an authorised recording, score or a teacher. Audio playback is enough to begin listening; recording yourself is optional. A backing track without a lead melody is useful later but may not teach an unfamiliar tune. If the reference is unavailable or unclear, obtain a usable version before guessing the melody.

Use a quiet place and an easy vocal volume. Do not begin vocal work while hoarse, painful, unusually tired or ill with voice changes. Stop for strain, new hoarseness, pain or uncertain comfort; listening and planning can continue silently. Do not force range extremes, imitate a singer's rasp, hold your breath or repeat until fatigued. Persistent voice problems call for clinical advice. These [NIDCD boundaries](../references/nidcd-2025-taking-care-of-your-voice.md) do not provide individual medical clearance. A teacher can help choose musical demands; this document does not prescribe register training or rehabilitation.

Stage order is **recommended**. The actual **prerequisites** for vocal work are a usable reference and comfortable vocal use; earlier exercises are optional support. Revisit a stage when the observed difficulty calls for it.

## Stage1 Choose the song and version

1. Choose the song, recording or arrangement, and setting: for example, singing at home with accompaniment. Record the link or edition so later comparisons concern the same version. Ben E. King's [recording link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TJx9E-rUqhg) is an orientation option; [Rhino's artist page](https://www.rhino.com/artist/ben-e-king) also points to an official playlist. Playback availability varies. A different available version is acceptable if you identify it.
2. Listen before singing. Notice passages that stay high or low, move quickly, contain unfamiliar words or leave little time between phrases. If comfortable, sample a short ordinary phrase and a potentially demanding one at easy volume, with a pause between them. You need not attempt the highest note to prove it is difficult. Mark comfort yes, unsure or no; stop vocal sampling for unsure or no.
3. Choose a workable version or get help adapting it. **Transposition** moves the melody and accompaniment together to a different pitch level while preserving their interval relationships. Lowering may ease high passages but make low ones less usable. Check both parts rather than selecting a key from voice-type labels. If no version seems manageable, choose another song or seek individual guidance.
4. Set the first milestone: one connected verse and chorus, followed later by the complete selected arrangement. Record the key if known, otherwise the exact track/version and any pitch-shift setting. Do not invent a key. Keep melody references and accompaniment in that same key.

**Stand by Me choice:** [Rockschool's Grade 1 vocals listing](../references/rsl-undated-vocals-grade-1.md) establishes use of an educational arrangement. It does not establish that the original performance is an absolute-beginner task or that its key suits you. The published arrangement and the commercial recording may differ. Our selection is a starting proposal, not a prescribed purchase or exam pathway.

The [tonal organisation Model](../models/tonal-organisation.md) explains why consistent transposition retains musical relationships. It cannot determine a comfortable key from a person's description alone. Slowing playback and changing pitch are different operations: when reducing tempo, use a pitch-preserving option if available, or use a slower demonstration in the intended key.

## Stage2 Understand the whole song

1. Listen through the selected version without trying to copy every note. Mark where sections begin, using your own timestamps or score locations. Identify the entrance, verse, chorus, any instrumental passage or repeats, and the ending actually present. A verse commonly carries developing text; a chorus returns as a recognisable section. Do not assume every recording uses the same sequence.
2. Read the words from your own source and say what the song communicates in your own words. Resolve unfamiliar words through a reliable pronunciation example or teacher. Choose who you imagine addressing and what you want that person to understand.
3. Mark one phrase to learn and an audible cue before it. A cue could be a count-in, an accompaniment event or the preceding phrase. Listen across its beginning and end so the phrase has a place in the song.

For **Stand by Me**, an initial interpretation to consider is a request for another person's presence and support. Check that against the words you are using and choose your own emphasis. Use the first verse and chorus as your working section; mark their actual boundaries in your version. These are listening tasks, not supplied timing measurements. If you cannot locate a boundary or hear the melody clearly, get a demonstration rather than building on an uncertain map.

## Stage3 Learn a meaningful phrase

[Pitch](../concepts/pitch.md) means perceived highness or lowness; [intonation](../concepts/intonation.md) concerns accuracy against the intended notes. A melody combines successive pitches with timing. Getting its direction right alone does not establish the intended melody.

1. Listen to the chosen phrase, pause playback and attempt it at an easy volume if comfortable. Start with the words if they are manageable. The target is the phrase's notes and timing, not the recorded singer's tone colour or loudness.
2. Compare what you attempted with the same phrase reference. [Pitch relationships](../models/pitch-relationships.md) distinguishes the direction of a melody from its actual intervals: an up-and-down shape alone does not establish the same tune. [Beat and rhythm](../concepts/beat-and-rhythm.md) distinguishes the recurring pulse from where notes and silences fall. Notice whether the uncertainty concerns melody, timing, words or their combination.
3. If useful, record your attempt separately from reference playback and listen at normal speed. Without a recording, report what you remember and retain uncertainty where memory is unclear. A loud lead vocal can conceal your own contribution; a blended sing-along is limited evidence about your independent melody.
4. Choose one observation to act on in Stage4. If the phrase is manageable, connect it to its neighbour in Stage5. Stop vocal work for uncertain comfort even if the musical result seems correct.

For example, you might remember the opening verse phrase but lose its timing as you add the words. That is a specific comparison to investigate. It does not establish that you need a higher key, more breath or louder singing. Hearing and producing a target involve different functions in the [auditory vocal Model](../models/auditory-vocal-pitch-matching.md); one mismatch does not identify its cause.

## Stage4 Work on the observed difficulty

Choose the row that matches the observation. The alternatives are tools for this song, not exercises everyone must complete. After working on a part, restore its original words, timing and surrounding phrase to check whether the change helped.

| Observation | Action and reason for choosing it | What to check next |
|---|---|---|
| One note's intended height is unclear | Obtain that note from the chosen melody in the chosen key, through a clear demonstration or suitable instrument/score. Use [reference pitch comparison](../methods/reference-pitch-comparison.md): listen silently, make a brief comfortable attempt, pause, replay and judge your voice as approximately matching, higher, lower or uncertain. | Replay restores the target, not your previous attempt. If direction or memory is uncertain, seek clearer feedback; do not guess corrections. Return the note to the phrase. The bundled tone bank may not contain this song's note. |
| The melody is remembered but words disrupt timing | Speak the words in their musical rhythm while tapping the pulse. This temporarily removes the sung-pitch demand. Start with the relevant fragment, then restore singing. | Do syllables and rests occupy the intended places? Speech in ordinary conversational rhythm is not necessarily the song's rhythm. |
| The melody becomes unclear when words are added | Try the phrase on a comfortable syllable such as oo, keeping the intended notes, then restore the words. This simplifies text coordination; it is not a new tone ideal. | Does the melody remain recognisable with the words restored? Success on the syllable alone does not finish the phrase. |
| The whole phrase feels too fast to follow | Use a slower pitch-preserving reference or demonstration, or a shorter meaningful passage. Retain enough context to know the entrance and ending. | Return towards the intended tempo only while manageable. Slowing a passage can lengthen sustained sounds, so do not hold them beyond comfort. |
| Phrases work separately but the join fails | Practise the end of one phrase and the beginning of the next, including any intervening silence and entrance cue. | Does the join work without restarting the accompaniment? Is the next entrance correctly placed? |
| The target is understood but the passage feels uncomfortable | Stop vocal work. Reconsider the key, arrangement or song with suitable help. | A correct note does not override discomfort. Do not repeat a difficult passage harder to prove control. |

The separation and slower-practice choices adapt [Open Music Theory's teaching guidance](../references/open-music-theory-undated-sight-singing.md). They reduce a demand for inspection; they do not prove the cause of a difficulty or guarantee improvement.

**Constructed Stand by Me example:** Suppose your verse and chorus each seem manageable alone, but you enter the chorus late after the verse. Work on that join with the preceding accompaniment cue, rather than drilling unrelated notes. If it works slowly but fails at the intended pace, the transition still needs work at that pace. This describes a possible decision, not your performance or a known difficulty in every arrangement.

## Stage5 Connect phrases and communicate

1. Connect neighbouring phrases, then a section. Keep the pulse through rests. Practise the entrance and ending with the actual accompaniment, using an identified cue; do not assume every vocal entrance lands on the first beat. If accompaniment is unavailable, a teacher's cue or an identified starting note can support unaccompanied preparation, but coordination with a backing track remains untested.
2. Choose phrase boundaries and possible breath opportunities that leave the words intelligible. Take an ordinary comfortable breath when needed; do not force a lyric line into one breath because the recording appears to do so. If a phrase cannot be managed comfortably, stop and reconsider its demands with help. A written breath mark is a plan, not proof of suitable technique.
3. Choose an expressive intention for a phrase: which word carries the main message, and should the phrase feel reassuring, urgent or reflective? Explore that through clear words and manageable emphasis. Louder is not automatically more expressive. Initially omit decorative slides or extra notes that obscure the main melody, and record that as an intentional simplification.
4. Extend the verse–chorus milestone to the remaining sections of the selected arrangement. Listen for changed words or melody on repeats; do not assume they are identical. Decide the final entrance and ending with the accompaniment. Continue only while comfortable.

In our example, a verse can establish the situation and the chorus can carry the central appeal. Try to make that relationship understandable to a listener. That is one interpretation to explore, not a required imitation of Ben E. King's delivery. A planned contrast is not evidence that a listener actually heard it.

## Stage6 Perform and review

1. When the sections are manageable, perform the selected arrangement from its chosen start to finish. For a minor musical slip, try returning at the next known cue if comfortable; stop for vocal discomfort. Do not restart the whole song repeatedly to erase a mistake. If only the verse and chorus are ready, label the attempt as an excerpt.
2. Review a recording if available, or invite a listener to identify what they heard. Use specific locations rather than a single good/bad rating. Separate melody and rhythm, intelligible words, continuity and intended expression. Ask about uncertain points rather than requiring a lengthy questionnaire. Report comfort from your experience, not the recording.
3. Choose one next action from the evidence: revisit a phrase, work on a join, clarify a reference, reconsider the arrangement, or finish for today. A tuner may help with an isolated note; it does not assess the entire song's rhythm, diction, expression or vocal comfort.

A first preparation target is a complete, recognisable rendition of the chosen arrangement with mostly reliable notes and timing, understandable words, manageable continuity and a communicative intention. Identify unresolved passages instead of hiding them behind a score. An intentional simplification is judged against the declared arrangement; an unexplained mismatch is still something to investigate. One acceptable run does not establish reliable performance on another day or in another song.

Keep a brief private note in whatever form is useful:

```text
Song/version and key or track setting:
Scope attempted: phrase / verse and chorus / whole arrangement / listening only
What I noticed, with location:
Evidence: memory / recording / listener feedback; uncertainty:
Comfort and any reason for stopping:
One next action:
```

The note is optional presentation, not a required form. Keep recordings and personal information outside this public repository. No score, lyrics or commercial audio needs to be copied into it.
